মতামত

BNP's budget puts welfare at the centre…but can it deliver?

Salahuddin Ahmed Raihan 28 জুন 2026 মূল ইংরেজি পড়ুন ↗

Confronted by a fragile macroeconomic landscape characterized by stubbornly high inflation, rising unemployment, and the persistent aftershocks of global economic uncertainty, the newly formed Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP) government has unveiled an ambitious expenditure plan totaling 938,000 crore taka earlier this month. 

The fiscal framework is built upon an explicit ideological pledge to transform the country into a humane welfare state. However, the stark divergence between high-minded social aspirations and structural fiscal realities suggests that the administration's journey toward a sustainable welfare model will be fraught with systemic execution risks and severe funding constraints.

To revive an economy under deep distress, the finance ministry has devised a sequential strategy dubbed the "3R" framework: Recovery and Stabilization, Restoration, and Reconstruction. The immediate recovery phase seeks to halt the current macroeconomic slide and restore baseline stability over the next fiscal year. 

This is intended to lead into a three-year restoration phase aimed at reforming the domestic revenue architecture, catalyzing private sector investment, restructuring a fragile financial sector, diversifying export streams, and upgrading human capital. 

The final reconstruction phase envisions a deeper structural transformation over a five-year horizon, pivoting toward innovation-led expansion and inclusive development. 

For the fiscal year 2026–27, the state has set an optimistic gross domestic product growth target of 6.5% while pledging to bring consumer price inflation down to a more manageable 7.5%.

Recognizing that aggregate output growth yields little political capital if the broader populace remains marginalized, the budget purposefully shifts resources toward social infrastructure, specifically education, healthcare, and targeted safety nets. 

The education sector has been allocated 136,606 crore taka, roughly equivalent to 2% of national output. The proposed structural interventions are comprehensive, encompassing the modernization of primary schooling, the expansion of digital multimedia classrooms, the institutionalization of school midday meals, and the provision of digital tablets to instructional staff. 

Higher education research funding is slated for an increase to 226 crore taka, alongside the introduction of a mandatory third language and free undergraduate education for female students. 

Reality check

Yet these capital-intensive reforms risk being undermined by basic administrative deficits; many premier public schools in Dhaka still lack basic sanitation and adequate toilet facilities for female students, underscoring the gap between high-level policy design and grassroots execution.

A parallel expansion is planned for public healthcare, an area where low-income citizens have historically faced catastrophic out-of-pocket expenses and widespread systemic neglect. 

The public medical apparatus at the sub-district level has long been crippled by chronic deficits in bed capacity, functional equipment, and qualified personnel. The state now intends to upgrade all rural Upazila hospitals to 101-bed facilities while expanding the procurement of diagnostic machinery. 

The healthcare envelope is set at 69,409 crore taka, or 1.02% of gross domestic product, with an official long-term policy target of raising health expenditures to 5% of national output. 

Plans to recruit 100,000 community health workers, including 80,000 women, alongside the deployment of an digitized e-health card system, are designed to streamline patient care and minimize institutional harassment. Whether these measures can overcome embedded institutional corruption and bureaucratic inertia remains highly questionable.

Direct social protection forms the core of the administration's redistribution strategy. Bangladesh currently manages a fragmented portfolio of over 90 distinct social safety net programs, a system historically plagued by elite capture, duplicate distribution, and severe bureaucratic inefficiencies. 

The BNP government has signaled an intent to consolidate these initiatives, utilizing its signature Family Card system as the primary vehicle for targeted relief. The program, which previously covered roughly 60,000 female-headed households, has been allocated 14,500 crore taka to expand its reach to 4.1 million women. 

Concurrently, the budget increases both individual transfer amounts and total beneficiary numbers for old-age pensions, vulnerable widows, abandoned women, and disabled citizens. Financial support has also been directed toward the political vanguard of the recent regime change, with monthly stipends earmarked for the families of individuals killed or injured during the July popular movement.

Gender-focused fiscal allocations extend beyond direct cash transfers. The state plans to introduce dedicated, female-operated "Pink Buses" within the chaotic urban transport networks to mitigate pervasive harassment and enhance female labor mobility. 

Furthermore, the Mother and Child Assistance Programme is scheduled to provide monthly cash allowances of 850 taka to nearly 1.9 million mothers, complemented by the construction of 60 modern childcare centers in urban hubs. 

Prudent resource mobilization 

In total, the broader social protection sector has been budgeted at 144,338 crore taka, reflecting an attempt to protect vulnerable segments of the population from the immediate pain of structural economic adjustment.

Yet a budget is ultimately an exercise in resource mobilization, and it is on the revenue side that the government’s welfare state ambitions collide with harsh arithmetic. To finance its vast expenditure commitments, the state has set an aggressive revenue collection target of 695,000 crore taka. 

Given Bangladesh’s historically abysmal tax-to-GDP ratio and a structurally weak National Board of Revenue, achieving this target appears highly improbable. This structural revenue shortfall leaves the state reliant on massive deficit financing, with the projected fiscal deficit calculated at a substantial 243,000 crore taka.

To bridge this gap, the finance ministry intends to secure 109,850 crore taka from external concessionary lenders and is actively seeking a new financial stabilization package from the International Monetary Fund. 

However, external financing remains contingent on stringent structural conditionalities that may compel the government to cut the very subsidies and social spending it has promised to expand. Domestically, the government plans to borrow 112,000 crore taka from the local commercial banking sector. 

This heavy reliance on domestic bank borrowing risks triggering a severe crowding-out effect, restricting the flow of private credit, driving up commercial interest rates, and stifling the private sector investment required to sustain macroeconomic growth.

Deficit spending is an established feature of Bangladesh's fiscal landscape, and an economy supporting nearly 200 million people undeniably requires robust public outlays to meet basic human developmental needs. 

The BNP's budget represents a clear ideological attempt to construct a more inclusive economic framework. However, the ultimate viability of this welfare model hinges not on the rhetorical generosity of its allocations, but on the state's capacity to collect taxes, curb institutional waste, and maintain macroeconomic stability under severe credit constraints. 

Without fundamental structural reforms to the state's revenue-generating apparatus, the grand vision of a humane welfare state risks dissolving into inflationary pressures and unsustainable public debt.

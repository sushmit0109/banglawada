'What’s the point of studying?': How Bihari camp success stories began changing minds

Biharis once belonged to a land that birthed some of the most famous universities of the Indian subcontinent: Nalanda, Vikramshila, and Mithila

Noor Islam Pappu 24 আগস্ট 2026 মূল ইংরেজি পড়ুন ↗

নূর ইসলাম পাপ্পু

This take is neither about SSC results nor GPA 5 grading. Rather, it is an exploration of how the camp-based Bihari community generally perceives education, or rather perceived education, and how success stories within the dimly-lit hallways have blazed a different path altogether.

While recognising the value of education and respecting educated people is a deep tradition in many cultures, ours is a bit more complicated. Whenever someone passed a board exam, instead of receiving praise, they would hear:"(পড় কে কা হোগা?) শিক্ষিত হয়ে লাভ কি?"

This mindset of completely dismissing the power of education – among a people who originated from a region once known as the global epicenter of higher education – didn’t happen overnight.

Biharis once belonged to a land that birthed some of the most famous universities of the Indian subcontinent: Nalanda, Vikramshila, and Mithila, which attracted scholars from across Asia, including China, Korea, Tibet, and Japan.

To see a community with such a heritage relegated to a dark age is the tragic consequence of systemic neglect: a complete lack of recognition, zero basic rights, and a total absence of influential connections.

For instance, most students dropped out in the earlier days because they struggled with the curriculum – especially Bangla grammar and mathematics.

Yet, within this unsupportive environment, a small, resilient group of young girls and boys living in the camp somehow managed to stay in school, value education, and admire the educated.

Today, those same individuals hold respectable professional positions in broader Bangladeshi society. They managed to transition out of the “Bihari camp” space to earn a decent living, with a few still actively working to improve educational opportunities for camp-based communities across Bangladesh.

তখন, আমরা আমাদের সম্প্রদায়ের মধ্যে এবং মূলধারার উভয় ক্ষেত্রেই শিক্ষিত লোকদের সাথে দেখা করার জন্য গভীরভাবে উত্সাহী ছিলাম। জেনেভা ক্যাম্পে একটি পুরস্কার বিতরণী অনুষ্ঠানে আল-ফালাহ-এর নির্বাহী পরিচালক আহমেদ ইলিয়াসের সাথে আমার দেখা প্রথম উচ্চ শিক্ষিত বিহারি ভদ্রলোক।

My friend Khalid pointed at him and whispered in sheer admiration,“E chacha ko dekh raha hai na? British amal ka matric pass hai (See that uncle over there? He passed Matric back in the British period!)”That was the legendary, larger-than-life way people introduced anyone who had managed to clear their Matric or modern-day SSC exams.

অনেক বছর পরে, ঠিক সেই জায়গায়, আমি আরেকজন শিক্ষিত যুবক বিহারী ভদ্রলোকের সাথে দেখা করি, যিনি সদ্য স্নাতকোত্তর ডিগ্রি অর্জন করেছিলেন।

Naturally, we threw a small celebration for him at the Al-Falah Clinic. During his introduction, the host declared with total seriousness, “Look at him, he is a graduate now! If he stands before the President, even the President should stand up and salute him!”

I don’t know what gesture makes the most logical sense, but this was how our little group proudly honoured educated people. Outside our circle, admirers were rare; most people reserved their true respect for those who made quick money as barbers or butchers.

Until 1994, very few students sat for the school final exam due to severe poverty, lack of institutional recognition, and countless other obstacles. The rare few in Geneva Camp who did pass became instant local celebrities, inspiring the rest of us to actually open a textbook.

In fact, I heard that a few educated individuals from Geneva Camp even managed to land jobs with UN agencies and the US Embassy back in the ‘70s and ‘80s! This was a rarity back in the day.

On top of that, building a career through education felt nearly impossible without strong personal connections ("Mama-Chacha"), something almost nonexistent for Biharis.

This only reinforced the widespread belief that studying was useless—a mere "hobby"—especially when the local butcher or barber was making a small fortune and in high demand as a prospective groom! Even today, those who generate immediate income are often valued more than those with degrees.

The stuff of legends

The legendary stories of those few individuals who passed the SSC and landed jobs at the UN and the US Embassy in the ‘70s and ‘80s were our ultimate benchmarks.

So, when I finally passed my school exams in 1994, my parents and I genuinely believed I was going to be the family’s ultimate game-changer – that I would not only secure a prestigious job but also elevate our social status. Reality, however, had both comic and cruel plans.

I neatly tucked in my shirt and marched off to several job interviews, only to fail miserably.

At a few places, employers literally laughed in my face, asking why a tiny, skinny kid who had just finished high school was begging for a job. I didn’t fully grasp the humiliation back then, but I quickly realised that applying for a formal job right after secondary school was not treated seriously– even though I was simply trying to make a living.

Around the same time in our neighbourhood, a boy my age was making waves.

সে আসলে স্কুলের পরীক্ষায় ফেল করেছিল, তবুও মতিঝিলে অফিসের হেলপার হিসেবে একটা আরামদায়ক চাকরি পেয়েছে! তিনি সপ্তাহে একবার বৃহস্পতিবার সন্ধ্যায় তার বাবা-মায়ের সাথে দেখা করতে ক্যাম্পে ফিরে আসেন। তিনি আমাদের সমবয়সী গ্রুপের পরম ভাগ্যবান লোক হিসাবে পালিত হয়েছিল। একদিন, আমি তাকে আমার হাতে লেখা সিভি দিলাম, এবং সে আমার জন্য একটি চাকরি খোঁজার প্রতিশ্রুতি দিল। কয়েক মাস ধরে, আমি প্রতি বৃহস্পতিবার তার জন্য অপেক্ষা করতাম, তার গলির প্রবেশদ্বারে মূর্তির মতো বিশ্বস্তভাবে দাঁড়িয়ে শুধু তাকে ধরতে এবং খবর জানতে চাইতাম। কিন্তু তার কোনোটাই ছিল না।

One Thursday, I was waiting as usual, but he didn’t show up. Later, a friend revealed that he had started taking a completely different, roundabout route home just to avoid me! My relentless waiting was giving the poor guy severe anxiety because he couldn’t find me a job.

After learning that, I stopped stalking him and took up odd jobs instead – working as a shopkeeper and later upgrading to a sewing-machine mechanic. Eventually, I pursued my studies privately, completed my graduation, and ultimately earned a Master’s degree from Sweden. How? Well, that is a long story for another day!

ইউএনএইচসিআর-এর মতে, বর্তমানে বাংলাদেশে ১১৬টি বিহারি ক্যাম্প রয়েছে।

Without official demographic data, it is nearly impossible to count exactly how many camp students take or pass the SSC exam each year. But given our population size, the numbers are certainly growing—and as social media posts demonstrate, a significant number of bright students are passing each year.

Whether people accept it or not, the camp-based Bihari community has gradually integrated into broader Bangladeshi society after living in squalid conditions for generations. Access to education has improved, particularly after a historic court ruling granted Biharis full citizenship rights. Many camp youths are striving to study, supported by community-based NGOs run by expats from our own community.

Interestingly, girls are leading the numbers in taking the exam – though, ironically, the underlying motivation for many families remains securing a better marriage proposal rather than building an independent career.

Compared to the ‘80s and ‘90s, today’s camp students have far better opportunities. Back then, schools outright refused to admit students who listed a camp address. Until 1994, no student could even sit for the final exam under an honest Bihari identity.

To bypass this, ambitious parents enrolled their children in nearby schools using fake addresses outside the camps.

In those early days, World Vision sponsored a few bright minds with tuition fees and notebooks, but major NGO interventions didn't exist. Over time, NGOs and government bodies stepped up, allowing camp students to register using their actual addresses.

The students motivated to continue today are mostly self-driven, though many receive a strong push from former camp residents who left during and after the 1971 war, settled in Western countries, received an education, and built successful lives. They began sending money back home to fund local educational initiatives through wonderful community organizations like OBAT, CoM, FoH, and many others.

বাস্তবতা এখনো রূঢ়

The raw, unvarnished reality persists: a severe lack of structured career paths and a fiercely competitive job market that rarely favors this marginalised community.

Mindsets toward education may have shifted over time, but deep-seated structural challenges remain.

A few years ago, I asked my friend Sajid – whose son was about to sit for the SSC exam from Geneva Camp – what his expectations were. His answer was gloriously grounded in reality: “If he passes, he can become anything. If he fails, well, at least he can drive a CNG or repair a CNG! Who counts whether my son passes or fails anyway? At the end of the day, we have to earn our rice and daal.” Having a backup plan for one's livelihood is practical, but it raises a profound question: Is surviving on the margins enough, or do we deserve a life of true dignity?

এখনও আশা আছে যে আমাদের সম্প্রদায় বুদ্ধিবৃত্তিক মানচিত্রে আমাদের ভাগ করা, গৌরবময় ঐতিহ্য পুনরুদ্ধার করতে ছাই থেকে উঠে আসবে। সর্বোপরি, আমাদের শিকড়গুলি বোধগয়ায় ফিরে আসে - সেই পবিত্র ভূমি যেখানে যুবরাজ সিদ্ধার্থকে স্বাগত জানানো হয়েছিল, যেখানে তিনি জ্ঞান অর্জন করেছিলেন এবং যেখানে তিনি বুদ্ধ হয়েছিলেন।

নূর ইসলাম পাপ্পুis a researcher and National Coordinator for Dalarna University and the University of Gothenburg. He specializes in community-ethnography and personal narrative storytelling based on his lived experiences.

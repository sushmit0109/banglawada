Rescue window narrows as Venezuela enters third day after twin quakes

UNB/AP 27 জুন 2026 মূল ইংরেজি পড়ুন ↗

The situation in Venezuela grew more desperate by the hour as people searched through the rubble of collapsed homes and apartment blocks three days after twin earthquakes measuring 7.2 and 7.5 struck the country, knowing time was running out to find survivors.

Authorities announced on Friday night that access to La Guaira, the epicentre of the devastation, would be restricted after heavy traffic and growing chaos hampered rescue operations. 

Officials said anyone wishing to enter the area would now require official permission, though they gave few details about who would be granted access. More than 14,000 military personnel and police officers have been deployed across the disaster zone, Acting President Delcy Rodríguez said on state television on Saturday.

With many complaining of an inadequate government response, Venezuelans took the search for missing relatives into their own hands. The death toll from Wednesday's earthquakes has climbed to at least 920, while more than 51,000 people remain unaccounted for. Residents in the worst-hit areas said they had seen few official rescue teams, despite authorities portraying the government's response as extensive.

Aid agencies regard the first 48 to 72 hours after a disaster as the critical window for finding survivors, although that period can be extended if those trapped have access to food and water.

"Each person saved is a miracle," said Jorge Rodríguez, president of the National Assembly. "We are not going to hide absolutely anything about the magnitude of this tragedy."

Families cling to hope as rescue efforts continue

In La Guaira state, just north of the capital Caracas, Nazareth Jiménez broke down in tears as she watched neighbours use hammers and power tools to cut through huge slabs of concrete at a residential building reduced to a mountain of rubble. She anxiously waited for news of her siblings, nieces, nephews and friends.

"My God, how are we going to get them out of there?" Jiménez whispered.

"We're making an appeal for help to the government and countries around the world," she said, pleading for heavy machinery capable of lifting collapsed structures. "There are still people alive in there."

Government forces distributed food and drinking water to survivors in La Guaira, while Rodríguez insisted the authorities were mounting a full-scale response during what she described as the "critical hours for rescuing people alive".

The catastrophe presents an enormous challenge for Rodríguez, the former vice-president who assumed office in January after former president Nicolás Maduro was captured and removed by the United States. Venezuela has endured more than a decade of economic turmoil, and many citizens reject the political movement Rodríguez now leads.

The death toll is expected to rise further. Tens of thousands of people have also been reported missing on independent online databases, although officials cautioned that many may simply be unreachable because mobile phone networks remain down, while duplicate reports are also likely.

As of Friday afternoon, more than 3,300 people had been injured and 243 survivors had been rescued, according to authorities.

Millions affected as international aid arrives

The International Organization for Migration estimated that as many as 6.76 million people could be affected by the disaster, including around 2 million in Caracas. Seismologists said the destruction was intensified by the rapid succession of two shallow earthquakes.

Loyce Pace, the International Red Cross' regional director for the Americas, said many survivors remained too frightened to return to what had once been their homes.

"People are still terrified to re-enter what were their homes," she said.

Many spent another night sleeping outdoors.

Omar Reyes, who said around 20 members of his extended family had died, wandered through the wreckage where two of his children remained buried.

"I've been left alone in this life," Reyes said.

In the city of Maiquetía, residents queued outside shops and pharmacies, where customers were admitted one at a time behind locked doors. At one point, a woman threw herself to the ground to shield a packet of nappies with her body, desperate not to lose it.

Congested roads and large numbers of motorcyclists repeatedly disrupted rescue operations. Mexican soldiers and volunteers repeatedly called for silence so they could listen for signs of life beneath the rubble, but civilian and military riders continued sounding horns and revving engines, much to rescuers' frustration.

Elsewhere, some people began carrying away essential goods, including food and toilet paper, from shops in Catia La Mar, near the country's main airport. Others crowded around a civilian pickup truck distributing bread and water until a soldier restored order. The car park of a pharmacy was transformed into a makeshift shelter filled with tarpaulins, hammocks and tents.

A few miles away, 28-year-old Yuleidy Cadenas stood opposite a collapsed public housing block, hoping rescue workers would find her son, mother and brother alive.

She had fled barefoot from another building as it collapsed during Wednesday's quake, only to discover that her mother's 12-storey apartment block had pancaked.

"I climbed onto the rubble and shouted for them to answer me, but nobody did — not my brother, not my son and not my mother," Cadenas said.

International assistance has continued to arrive. Venezuelan authorities said on Friday that 861 volunteers from Mexico, the United States, El Salvador, Switzerland, Colombia and several other countries were already in the country, with more expected in the coming days.

Acting President Rodríguez also said she had spoken with US President Donald Trump and Secretary of State Marco Rubio on Friday, and that both had reaffirmed Washington's commitment to sending additional rescue teams and emergency equipment.

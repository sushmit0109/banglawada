VAT pressure mounts on footwear industry as manufacturers warn of “severe strain”

Staff Correspondent 4 মে 2026 মূল ইংরেজি পড়ুন ↗

Bangladesh’s footwear sector is coming under intensifying pressure from value-added tax (VAT), with industry leaders warning in a recent press conference that the current tax regime is choking growth and undermining competitiveness.

At the press briefing—organised by sector representatives—manufacturers laid out a stark assessment of the situation.

They argued that VAT is being applied in a way that inflates production costs at multiple stages, leaving firms squeezed between rising expenses and weak market demand.

One industry representative said the sector is under extreme pressure, reflecting growing alarm within the business community.

The complaints centre on what producers describe as a cascading tax burden. VAT is imposed not only at the final stage but also across inputs and intermediate processes, effectively compounding costs.

As a result, locally produced footwear is becoming more expensive, eroding its position both at home and abroad.

Industry leaders used the press conference to highlight the mismatch between policy ambition and fiscal reality. While the government has repeatedly identified leather and footwear as priority export sectors, manufacturers argue that tax policy is working in the opposite direction.

“The current VAT structure is not industry-friendly”, one speaker noted, calling for urgent reform.

The impact is already visible. Smaller manufacturers—who dominate the domestic footwear market—are reportedly the hardest hit. With limited capital buffers, many are struggling to absorb higher costs, forcing them to either cut production or pass price increases onto consumers.

Larger exporters, meanwhile, face tightening margins in international markets where buyers are highly price-sensitive.

The press conference also underscored growing concerns about competitiveness. Bangladesh’s footwear exports have long lagged behind potential, especially compared with regional peers such as Vietnam and India.

Industry representatives warned that higher tax-induced costs could further weaken the country’s position in global supply chains, making it harder to attract orders.

Manufacturers also cautioned that excessive taxation could fuel informality. When compliance costs rise, smaller players may be pushed outside the formal system, undermining fair competition and eroding the tax base itself. This, they argued, would be a counterproductive outcome for both the industry and the government.
